## 1. General Disaster Preparedness Principles

- **Before**: Know your area's risk (flood zone, earthquake zone, cyclone-prone coast, landslide-prone hill), keep an emergency kit ready, know evacuation routes and nearest shelters, keep important documents in a waterproof/fireproof pouch, save emergency numbers.
- **During**: Follow official alerts (never rumors), move to safety immediately rather than collecting belongings, stay calm, help vulnerable people nearby (elderly, children, disabled, pregnant women) if it's safe to do so.
- **After**: Do not return home until authorities declare it safe, watch for secondary hazards (aftershocks, disease outbreaks, contaminated water, unstable structures), check on neighbors.

### Emergency Kit Checklist
- Drinking water (3 liters/person/day, 3-day supply)
- Non-perishable food (3-day supply)
- Flashlight + extra batteries / power bank
- First-aid kit and personal medications
- Whistle (to signal for help)
- Dust masks, plastic sheeting, duct tape
- Battery or hand-crank radio
- Cash in small denominations
- Copies of ID, insurance, medical records (waterproof bag)
- Local maps, phone with offline maps downloaded

## 2. Floods and Flash Floods

Flash floods are the fastest-developing and often deadliest flood type — they can occur within minutes to hours of heavy rainfall, dam failure, or glacial lake outburst, especially in hilly/mountainous terrain like Northeastern India.

### Warning Signs of an Imminent Flash Flood
- Sudden rise in a normally calm stream or river
- Unusual rumbling or roaring sound from upstream (rocks/debris moving)
- Rapid muddying of previously clear water
- Debris (branches, foam) floating past faster than normal
- Heavy, sustained rainfall upstream even if it isn't raining locally
- Water levels rising at nearby dams/reservoirs reported by authorities

### What To Do Before
- Identify high ground near your home, workplace, and school in advance
- Avoid building or staying in homes near stream banks, dry creek beds, or steep slopes
- Keep a battery-powered radio for alerts when power/network fails
- Sign up for local government flood alert systems if available

### What To Do During
- **Move to higher ground immediately** — do not wait for official orders if water is rising fast
- **Never walk, swim, or drive through flood water.** Just 15 cm (6 inches) of fast-moving water can knock an adult down; 30 cm (1 foot) can sweep away a car.
- If trapped in a building, move to the highest level (but not a closed attic — risk of being trapped as water rises)
- If your vehicle is caught in rising water, abandon it and move to higher ground on foot if it's safe to do so
- Stay away from downed power lines and any water that may be electrically charged
- Avoid crossing bridges over fast-moving flood water

### What To Do After
- Avoid flood water — it may be contaminated with sewage, chemicals, or hide debris
- Watch for washed-out roads, weakened bridges, and sinkholes
- Do not drink tap water until authorities confirm it's safe
- Discard food that has come into contact with flood water
- Check structures for damage before re-entering (cracks, tilting, weakened foundations)
- Beware of landslides in hilly areas for days after heavy rain (saturated soil remains unstable)

### Region Note — Northeastern India
- Monsoon season (roughly June–September) and pre-monsoon storms bring the highest flash flood risk
- Rivers fed by Himalayan/hill catchments can rise very fast; upstream rain matters as much as local rain
- Landslide risk is closely linked to flash floods in this terrain — heavy rain can trigger both simultaneously
- Community-reported anomalies (unusual water color, sound, animal behavior) are valuable early indicators where formal sensor coverage is sparse

## 3. Earthquakes

### During
- **Drop, Cover, Hold On**: Drop to hands and knees, take cover under sturdy furniture, hold on until shaking stops
- If indoors, stay indoors; move away from windows, mirrors, and heavy furniture that could fall
- If outdoors, move to an open area away from buildings, trees, and power lines
- If driving, pull over away from bridges/overpasses and stay in the vehicle until shaking stops

### After
- Expect aftershocks
- Check yourself and others for injuries before helping others
- Check for gas leaks (smell, hissing) — if suspected, turn off the main gas valve and leave immediately
- Do not use elevators
- Inspect your home for structural damage before staying inside

---

## 4. Cyclones / Hurricanes

### Before
- Secure loose outdoor items, reinforce windows/doors
- Know your evacuation zone and route
- Charge devices and fill water containers in advance

### During
- Stay indoors, away from windows, in the strongest part of the building (often an interior room on the lowest floor, but above expected flood level)
- If told to evacuate, do so immediately — do not wait
- Beware the "eye" of the storm — calm weather is temporary, winds will resume from the opposite direction

### After
- Stay away from downed power lines and flooded areas
- Watch for storm surge and flooding even after winds die down

---

## 5. Landslides

### Warning Signs
- New cracks in ground, walls, or pavement
- Doors or windows sticking for the first time
- Tilting trees, poles, or fences
- Unusual sounds like cracking trees or boulders knocking together
- A faint rumbling sound that increases as the landslide nears

### During
- Move away quickly, perpendicular to the direction of the slide (sideways, not straight downhill)
- If escape isn't possible, curl into a tight ball and protect your head

---

## 6. Wildfires

### During
- Evacuate immediately if told to; don't wait to see flames
- If trapped, call for help, stay low, cover mouth/nose with a damp cloth
- Keep doors closed but unlocked (for firefighter access) if sheltering in place is the only option

---

## 7. Heatwaves

- Stay hydrated, avoid outdoor activity during peak heat (usually noon–4 PM)
- Wear light, loose clothing; use ORS if sweating heavily
- Watch for heat exhaustion (heavy sweating, weakness, nausea) and heat stroke (high body temp, confusion, no sweating) — heat stroke is a medical emergency

---

## 8. Universal Emergency Response Guidance

### SOS / Distress Signals
- Three of anything (three whistle blasts, three fires, three flashes) is a universal distress signal
- If phone network is down, try SMS (often works when calls don't) or emergency broadcast channels

### Helping Others Safely
- Never put yourself at greater risk to help someone else
- Check for danger before approaching an injured person
- Basic first aid: control bleeding with direct pressure, keep injured person still if spinal injury suspected, keep unconscious but breathing person in the recovery position

### Communicating With an AI Disaster Assistant (design notes for this model)
- Prioritize immediate safety actions over general information when a user describes an active, unfolding disaster
- Ask for location context when possible to give locally relevant guidance (nearest shelter, region-specific hazards)
- Never delay safety-critical advice to ask unnecessary clarifying questions
- Always recommend contacting local emergency services / authorities as the authoritative source, especially for SOS situations
- Be calm, direct, and concise in tone during active emergencies; more explanatory in preparedness/precaution conversations
